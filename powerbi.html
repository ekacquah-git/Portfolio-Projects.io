Power BI

Reporting Built for Decisions

Power BI is where the work becomes a tool someone actually uses. I build reports the way I used to build operator interfaces: the screen should tell you where to look before you have read a single number. The design sits on a neutral base, and color gets used deliberately. When a number needs attention, it is the only element on the page carrying color, so the eye finds it without anyone pointing. A report where everything competes for attention is a report where nothing gets it.

That habit comes from controls engineering. An operator watching dozens of values at once cannot evaluate all of them in real time, so the interface does the evaluating and surfaces only the exceptions. The same principle holds for a P&L review or a compliance dashboard. Conditional formatting on variance, buffer thresholds that flag risk ahead of a breach, and watch lists that surface the bottom performers all do the same job. They move the analytical judgment into the model so the meeting can be about decisions.

FP&A Analytics

Budget vs. Actuals and P&L Reporting

The goal was to give a Director of FP&A one place to run a monthly business review, hold departments to their budgets, and see where the full year was tracking. Three fiscal years of actuals and budget data across departments and GL accounts, answering three questions: which departments are over or under and by how much, where the P&L lands by year end, and which cost categories are driving the variance.

The purpose it served was making the monthly review a conversation about exceptions. The variance columns carry the conditional formatting, so a director opens the executive page and the departments that need discussion are already marked. Everything within tolerance stays neutral and quiet. That is the whole design intent: the report does the first pass of analysis so the meeting can start at the second.

Architecture: A star schema with two fact tables sharing three dimensions. Budget and actuals stay separate rather than getting unioned, because they arrive at different grain and refresh on different schedules, and collapsing them creates a dependency that breaks the moment one source is late. A P&L sort order calculated column on the accounts dimension enforces income statement sequence across every visual, which matters because income statement order is not alphabetical and a finance audience notices immediately.

Data quality: Actuals needed sign reversal on expense accounts, duplicate transaction IDs removed, and date formats reconciled. Budget rows were missing department IDs that had to be recovered through a lookup join, and zero-amount allocation rows had to be handled deliberately so they stayed visible without distorting the variance math.

Three report pages: An executive P&L dashboard with full income statement, budget against actual variance, and YTD trend for the monthly review. A department budget tracking page with drill-through, showing each department's spend against allocation by account category. And a forecast page projecting the full year from actuals to date plus remaining budget, with run rate against annual target.

Manufacturing and Supply Chain

Operations Analytics Platform

Operations teams usually track quality and supply chain separately, which makes it hard to see how one affects the other. I built this platform to put both in the same place. It runs on a three-schema setup in PostgreSQL: raw holds the landing zone, core holds the cleaned views, and mart holds the pre-aggregated tables that Power BI actually connects to.

The quality view. First pass yield lands at 79.4% against an 85% target, and the escaped defect rate at 6.3% sits above the 5% threshold. Breaking yield out by product line is what makes the number actionable: Drone at 67.6% and Fixed at 70.6% are dragging the overall figure down, while CEW at 83.7% is close to target. An aggregate of 79.4% tells you there is a problem. The breakdown tells you where it lives.

The supply chain view. On-time delivery at 63.0% across 19 active suppliers, with average lead time running two days past standard. Ranking by supplier and grouping by tier surfaces which relationships are carrying the delay rather than leaving it as one blended rate.

I profiled the data before transforming any of it. Duplicates, nulls, mixed values, and orphaned foreign keys all got documented and triaged first, because finding those later means rebuilding. The interesting part came out of joining the two domains: late deliveries from Tier 2 suppliers lined up with higher defect rates on the production lines downstream. Neither dashboard shows that on its own, which is the argument for building both off one model.

Supply Chain · Fulfillment Performance

Logistics and fulfillment analytics covering shipment tracking, freight costs, on-time delivery, and how carriers compare against each other. The model runs on two fact tables, roughly 5,000 shipments and 5,060 orders, joined out to four dimensions. The date table was built in Power Query across 2023 to 2025 and marked properly so time intelligence works the way it should.

Before any of that worked I had to deal with the data. Duplicate order line IDs, negative freight costs, fulfillment anomalies, and orphaned foreign keys all got handled and documented first. Then I added the columns that make the analysis possible: delivery days, an on-time flag, and freight cost per unit. Three pages came out of it, covering executive KPIs, freight cost broken down by carrier and lane, and delivery performance with drill-through.

Supply Chain · Salesforce Vendor Performance

A vendor performance dashboard built from Salesforce CRM exports, pulling together four sources: PO line items, the vendor master, supply disruption cases, and products. This is the kind of build you end up doing when Salesforce is where the operational data actually lives.

The metric that matters here is the case-to-PO ratio. A vendor with a lot of disruption cases is not necessarily a problem if they are also handling a lot of volume, so measuring cases against purchase order count is what separates a genuinely risky vendor from a busy one. Getting there meant deduplicating records, flagging negative unit costs, documenting quantity anomalies, and standardizing vendor ID casing so the joins would hold.

Retail Analytics

Adventure Works Sales and Customer Analytics

A full Power BI build on the Adventure Works retail dataset, worked through in five stages: cleaning in Power Query, building the model, writing the DAX, putting the visuals together, and finally letting Key Influencers and Decomposition Tree surface patterns on their own. I built it in stages on purpose so each layer had to hold up before the next one went on top of it.

The model is a star schema covering three years of sales alongside customer, product, territory, returns, and calendar dimensions. From there it answers the questions a retail team actually asks: where revenue and profit are coming from, which customers carry the most weight, what is getting returned, and how any of it compares to last year.

Maven Market Retail Analytics

A Power BI build on the Maven Market grocery dataset, covering two years of transactions across 1997 and 1998. The interesting wrinkle here is two separate fact tables feeding one star schema, joined out to store, product, customer, regional, returns, and calendar dimensions.

What it surfaces is the stuff a grocery chain runs on. How each store and region is performing, what customers keep coming back for, which categories are getting returned, and how any store stacks up against the rest of the chain year over year.
